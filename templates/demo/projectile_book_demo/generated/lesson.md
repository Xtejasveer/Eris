# What a projectile is
NOTE: What is a projectile?
- thrown or launched
- then moves under gravity alone
END
Let's start with a fielder throwing the ball to the keeper. Once it leaves the hand, the only thing acting on it is gravity. We are ignoring air resistance. That is a projectile: anything thrown or launched that then moves under gravity alone. A football kick is one too. So is the arc of water from a hose, where every drop follows the same curved path. Open your book at page 38, where this definition sits. Make sense?

# Two motions at the same time: horizontal and vertical are independent
NOTE: Two motions at the same time
- sideways: no push, so the speed stays the same
- up and down: gravity, g = 9.8 m/s²
END
Here are the two motions. Think of a ball hit from the boundary. Sideways, nothing pushes or pulls it, so its horizontal speed stays the same all the way. Up and down, gravity acts on it, pulling down at 9.8 metres per second every second. The book gives this in Equation 3.35: ax is zero, and ay is minus g. The up-and-down motion is the one that slows going up and speeds up coming down. So the two motions happen at the same time but never mix. Each direction can be solved on its own, with its own simple rule. That is the big idea: horizontal and vertical motion are independent. Clear so far?

# Splitting the launch speed: ux = u cos θ and uy = u sin θ
When the ball leaves the hand at an angle, the launch speed u has two parts. Here u is the launch speed and θ is the angle above the horizontal. Take u = 20 m/s at 30 degrees. Here is the picture.
SKETCH: box:Launch speed u | arrow:split | result:ux and uy
Here is the rule for the sideways part, ux = u cos θ. Let's put in the numbers.
NOTE:
- ux = u cos θ = 20 × cos 30° = 17.3 m/s
END
So the sideways speed is 17.3 m/s.
SKETCH: box:ux = u cos θ | good:sideways, steady
Next is the upward part, uy = u sin θ.
NOTE:
- uy = u sin θ = 20 × sin 30° = 10 m/s
END
So the upward speed is 10 m/s, and this is the part gravity will slow down.
SKETCH: box:uy = u sin θ | good:upwards, changes
Common mistake: using 20 m/s for both directions. The full speed does not go sideways or up. Takeaway: split the launch speed first, then use each part on its own.

# Formulas for height, time and range
FIGURE How to solve any projectile problem (steps)
Here are the steps for any projectile problem. First, write down what is given: u, θ and g. Second, split u into ux and uy, as we just did. Third, the maximum height is h = uy² over 2g. Fourth, the time of flight is T = 2 uy over g. Fifth, the range is R = u² sin 2θ over g. Sixth, check the answer against the book.
NOTE:
- h = uy² / 2g = 10² / (2 × 9.8)
END
Let's use our 20 m/s example, where uy is 10 m/s, and work the height first.
This is the formula with our numbers in place. Now the top of the fraction.
NOTE:
- 10² = 100
END
Ten squared is 100, so the top of the fraction is 100.
NOTE:
- 2 × 9.8 = 19.6
END
Two times 9.8 is 19.6, so the bottom is 19.6.
NOTE:
- h = 100 / 19.6 = 5.1 m
END
100 divided by 19.6 gives 5.1, so the maximum height is 5.1 m, the highest point of the throw. Why uy and not u? Gravity only acts up and down, so only the upward part matters for the height. Next, the time in the air, T = 2 uy over g.
NOTE:
- 2 × 10 = 20
END
Twice uy is 20, which goes on the top.
NOTE:
- T = 20 / 9.8 = 2.04 s
END
20 divided by 9.8 gives 2.04, so the time in the air is 2.04 s. Going up and coming down take the same time, because the path is symmetrical. Now the range, R = u² sin 2θ over g. We can also multiply the sideways speed by the time in the air.
NOTE:
- R = ux × T = 17.3 × 2.04 = 35.3 m
END
17.3 times 2.04 gives 35.3, so the range is 35.3 m. The shortcut uses u squared and sin 2θ, so let's check it. Here are the first two pieces.
NOTE:
- u² = 400, and sin 60° = 0.866
END
u squared is 400, and sin 60 is 0.866.
NOTE:
- R = 400 × 0.866 / 9.8 = 346.4 / 9.8 = 35.3 m
END
400 times 0.866 is 346.4, and 346.4 divided by 9.8 gives 35.3 again. Both routes agree, so the shortcut is a good check. Takeaway: height and time come from uy, and the range comes from the sideways speed times the time in the air, or from the shortcut.

# NCERT Example 3.8
This is Example 3.8 on page 40 of your book. A cricket ball is thrown at 28 m/s, 30 degrees above the horizontal. We need three answers: the maximum height, the time to return to the same level, and the distance. Write the givens first.
NOTE: Example 3.8 givens
- u = 28 m/s, θ = 30°
- g = 9.8 m/s²
END
Those are our starting numbers. Part (a) is the height, and it needs uy first.
NOTE:
- uy = 28 × sin 30° = 14 m/s
END
So uy is 14 m/s, the upward part of the throw. Now the height, h = uy² over 2g.
NOTE:
- 14² = 196
END
14 squared is 196, which goes on the top.
NOTE:
- h = 196 / 19.6 = 10.0 m
END
196 divided by 19.6 gives 10.0, so the maximum height is 10.0 m. Part (b) is the time to return to the same level, T = 2 uy over g.
NOTE:
- 2 × 14 = 28
END
Twice 14 is 28.
NOTE:
- T = 28 / 9.8 = 2.86, about 2.9 s
END
28 divided by 9.8 gives 2.86, which rounds to about 2.9 s. Part (c) is the distance, R = u² sin 2θ over g, with 2θ equal to 60 degrees. Let's get the pieces first.
NOTE:
- 28² = 784, and sin 60° = 0.866
END
28 squared is 784, and sin 60 is 0.866.
NOTE:
- 784 × 0.866 = 678.9
END
784 times 0.866 gives 678.9, so that is the top of the range fraction.
NOTE:
- R = 678.9 / 9.8 = 69.3 m
END
678.9 divided by 9.8 gives 69.3, so the distance is about 69 m. That gives 10.0 m, 2.9 s and 69 m, which matches the book's answers on page 40. Now let's check it with code.
CODE checks_example_3_8.py
import math
g = 9.8
u = 28
theta = math.radians(30)
ux = u * math.cos(theta)
uy = u * math.sin(theta)
h = uy ** 2 / (2 * g)
T = 2 * uy / g
R = ux * T
print("Maximum height:", round(h, 1), "m")
print("Time of flight:", round(T, 1), "s")
print("Range:", round(R), "m")
END
Here is the program that checks these three numbers. Let's run it and see.
OUTPUT

Time of flight: 2.9 s
Range: 69 m
END
Good, the program printed three lines: the maximum height, 10.0 m; the time of flight, 2.9 s; and the range, 69 m. They match our hand working and the book's answers on page 40.

# Why 45 degrees gives the maximum range
Look at the formula again: R = u² sin 2θ / g. For one throw, u and g stay fixed, so the only thing that changes is sin 2θ. Sine is largest, equal to 1, when its angle is 90 degrees. So 2θ must be 90 degrees, which makes θ equal to 45 degrees. Let's test it with two angles that add up to 90, starting with 15 degrees.
NOTE:
- 2 × 15° = 30°, and sin 30° = 0.5
END
Twice 15 is 30, and the sine of 30 is 0.5.
NOTE:
- R = 400 × 0.5 / 9.8 = 200 / 9.8 = 20.4 m
END
NOTE:
- 2 × 75° = 150°, and sin 150° = 0.5
END
400 times 0.5 is 200, and 200 divided by 9.8 gives 20.4. So 15 degrees gives 20.4 m. Now 75 degrees.
Twice 75 is 150, and the sine of 150 is also 0.5, the same value as before.
NOTE:
- R = 400 × 0.5 / 9.8 = 20.4 m
END
So 75 degrees also gives 20.4 m. Now the peak, 45 degrees.
NOTE:
- 2 × 45° = 90°, and sin 90° = 1
END
Twice 45 is 90, and the sine of 90 is exactly 1, its largest possible value.
NOTE:
- R = 400 × 1 / 9.8 = 40.8 m
END
400 divided by 9.8 gives 40.8, the largest range of the three angles. Takeaway: sin 2θ peaks at 90 degrees, so 45 degrees throws farthest, and angles that add up to 90 share the same range. Book Example 3.6 on page 40 proves this same symmetry, so compare its proof with our numbers.

# Recap
Our opening question was which angle throws farthest on flat ground. The answer is 45 degrees, because sin 2θ is largest there. Remember three things: horizontal and vertical motion are independent, split u into ux and uy first, and use R = u² sin 2θ / g for the range.
