[SECTION title="Two motions at the same time: horizontal and vertical are independent"]
[NOTE_TITLE text="Two motions at the same time"]
[NOTE]
- sideways: no push, so the speed stays the same
- up and down: gravity, g = 9.8 m/s²
[/NOTE]
[SLIDE n=3]
Here are the two motions. Think of a ball hit from the boundary. Sideways, nothing pushes or pulls it, so its horizontal speed stays the same all the way. Up and down, gravity acts on it, pulling down at 9.8 metres per second every second.
[BOOK page=38]
[BOOK_HIGHLIGHT page=38 text="ax = 0, ay = – g" while_speaking=true]
The book gives this in Equation 3.35: ax is zero, and ay is minus g. The up-and-down motion is the one that slows going up and speeds up coming down. So the two motions happen at the same time but never mix. Each direction can be solved on its own, with its own simple rule. That is the big idea: horizontal and vertical motion are independent. Clear so far?

[SECTION title="Splitting the launch speed: ux = u cos θ and uy = u sin θ"]
When the ball leaves the hand at an angle, the launch speed u has two parts. Here u is the launch speed and θ is the angle above the horizontal.
[SLIDE n=4]
Take u = 20 m/s at 30 degrees. Here is the picture.
[SKETCH_ROW items="box:Launch speed u | arrow:split | result:ux and uy"]
Here is the rule for the sideways part, ux = u cos θ. Let's put in the numbers.
[NOTE]
- ux = u cos θ = 20 × cos 30° = 17.3 m/s
[/NOTE]
[SKETCH_ROW items="box:ux = u cos θ | good:sideways, steady"]
So the sideways speed is 17.3 m/s.
Next is the upward part, uy = u sin θ.
[NOTE]
- uy = u sin θ = 20 × sin 30° = 10 m/s
[/NOTE]
[SKETCH_ROW items="box:uy = u sin θ | good:upwards, changes"]
So the upward speed is 10 m/s, and this is the part gravity will slow down.
Common mistake: using 20 m/s for both directions. The full speed does not go sideways or up. Takeaway: split the launch speed first, then use each part on its own.

[SECTION title="Formulas for height, time and range"]
[FLOW id="steps" title="How to solve any projectile problem" from="projectile_notes.docx How to solve any projectile problem (steps)"]
given: Write down what is given: u, θ and g
split: Split u into ux and uy
height: Maximum height: h = uy² / 2g
time: Time of flight: T = 2 uy / g
range: Range: R = u² sin 2θ / g
check: Check the answer against the book
[/FLOW]
Here are the steps for any projectile problem.
[FLOW_NEXT id="steps"]
First, write down what is given: u, θ and g.
[FLOW_NEXT id="steps"]
Second, split u into ux and uy, as we just did.
[FLOW_NEXT id="steps"]
Third, the maximum height is h = uy² over 2g.
[FLOW_NEXT id="steps"]
Fourth, the time of flight is T = 2 uy over g.
[FLOW_NEXT id="steps"]
Fifth, the range is R = u² sin 2θ over g.
[FLOW_NEXT id="steps"]
Sixth, check the answer against the book.
[NOTE]
- h = uy² / 2g = 10² / (2 × 9.8)
[/NOTE]
Let's use our 20 m/s example, where uy is 10 m/s, and work the height first.
This is the formula with our numbers in place. Now the top of the fraction.
[NOTE]
- 10² = 100
[/NOTE]
Ten squared is 100, so the top of the fraction is 100.
[NOTE]
- 2 × 9.8 = 19.6
[/NOTE]
Two times 9.8 is 19.6, so the bottom is 19.6.
[NOTE]
- h = 100 / 19.6 = 5.1 m
[/NOTE]
100 divided by 19.6 gives 5.1, so the maximum height is 5.1 m, the highest point of the throw. Why uy and not u? Gravity only acts up and down, so only the upward part matters for the height. Next, the time in the air, T = 2 uy over g.
[NOTE]
- 2 × 10 = 20
[/NOTE]
Twice uy is 20, which goes on the top.
[NOTE]
- T = 20 / 9.8 = 2.04 s
[/NOTE]
20 divided by 9.8 gives 2.04, so the time in the air is 2.04 s. Going up and coming down take the same time, because the path is symmetrical. Now the range, R = u² sin 2θ over g. We can also multiply the sideways speed by the time in the air.
[NOTE]
- R = ux × T = 17.3 × 2.04 = 35.3 m
[/NOTE]
17.3 times 2.04 gives 35.3, so the range is 35.3 m. The shortcut uses u squared and sin 2θ, so let's check it. Here are the first two pieces.
[NOTE]
- u² = 400, and sin 60° = 0.866
[/NOTE]
u squared is 400, and sin 60 is 0.866.
[NOTE]
- R = 400 × 0.866 / 9.8 = 346.4 / 9.8 = 35.3 m
[/NOTE]
400 times 0.866 is 346.4, and 346.4 divided by 9.8 gives 35.3 again. Both routes agree, so the shortcut is a good check. Takeaway: height and time come from uy, and the range comes from the sideways speed times the time in the air, or from the shortcut.

A cricket ball is thrown at 28 m/s, 30 degrees above the horizontal. We need three answers: the maximum height, the time to return to the same level, and the distance. Write the givens first.
[NOTE_TITLE text="Example 3.8 givens"]
[NOTE]
- u = 28 m/s, θ = 30°
- g = 9.8 m/s²
[/NOTE]
Those are our starting numbers. Part (a) is the height, and it needs uy first.
[NOTE]
- uy = 28 × sin 30° = 14 m/s
[/NOTE]
So uy is 14 m/s, the upward part of the throw. Now the height, h = uy² over 2g.
[NOTE]
- 14² = 196
[/NOTE]
14 squared is 196, which goes on the top.
[NOTE]
- h = 196 / 19.6 = 10.0 m
[/NOTE]
196 divided by 19.6 gives 10.0, so the maximum height is 10.0 m. Part (b) is the time to return to the same level, T = 2 uy over g.
[NOTE]
- 2 × 14 = 28
[/NOTE]
Twice 14 is 28.
[NOTE]
- T = 28 / 9.8 = 2.86, about 2.9 s
[/NOTE]
28 divided by 9.8 gives 2.86, which rounds to about 2.9 s. Part (c) is the distance, R = u² sin 2θ over g, with 2θ equal to 60 degrees. Let's get the pieces first.
[NOTE]
- 28² = 784, and sin 60° = 0.866
[/NOTE]
28 squared is 784, and sin 60 is 0.866.
[NOTE]
- 784 × 0.866 = 678.9
[/NOTE]
784 times 0.866 gives 678.9, so that is the top of the range fraction.
[NOTE]
- R = 678.9 / 9.8 = 69.3 m
[/NOTE]
678.9 divided by 9.8 gives 69.3, so the distance is about 69 m.
[BOOK_ANSWER page=40 name="Example 3.8"]
That gives 10.0 m, 2.9 s and 69 m, which matches the book's answers on page 40. Now let's check it with code.
[WRITE_CODE file="checks_example_3_8.py"]
import math
g = 9.8
u = 28
theta = math.radians(30)
ux = u * math.cos(theta)
uy = u * math.sin(theta)
h = uy ** 2 / (2 * g)
T = 2 * uy / g
R = ux * T
print("Maximum height:", round(h, 1), "m")
print("Time of flight:", round(T, 1), "s")
print("Range:", round(R), "m")
[/WRITE_CODE]
Here is the program that checks these three numbers. Let's run it and see.

[SECTION title="Why 45 degrees gives the maximum range"]
[SLIDE n=10]
Look at the formula again: R = u² sin 2θ / g. For one throw, u and g stay fixed, so the only thing that changes is sin 2θ. Sine is largest, equal to 1, when its angle is 90 degrees.
[SLIDE_HIGHLIGHT n=10 text="sin 2θ is largest (= 1) when 2θ = 90°, so θ = 45°." while_speaking=true]
So 2θ must be 90 degrees, which makes θ equal to 45 degrees. Let's test it with two angles that add up to 90, starting with 15 degrees.
[NOTE]
- 2 × 15° = 30°, and sin 30° = 0.5
[/NOTE]
Twice 15 is 30, and the sine of 30 is 0.5.
[NOTE]
- R = 400 × 0.5 / 9.8 = 200 / 9.8 = 20.4 m
[/NOTE]
400 times 0.5 is 200, and 200 divided by 9.8 gives 20.4. So 15 degrees gives 20.4 m. Now 75 degrees.
[NOTE]
- 2 × 75° = 150°, and sin 150° = 0.5
[/NOTE]
Twice 75 is 150, and the sine of 150 is also 0.5, the same value as before.
[NOTE]
- R = 400 × 0.5 / 9.8 = 20.4 m
[/NOTE]
So 75 degrees also gives 20.4 m. Now the peak, 45 degrees.
[NOTE]
- 2 × 45° = 90°, and sin 90° = 1
[/NOTE]
Twice 45 is 90, and the sine of 90 is exactly 1, its largest possible value.
[NOTE]
- R = 400 × 1 / 9.8 = 40.8 m
[/NOTE]
400 divided by 9.8 gives 40.8, the largest range of the three angles. Takeaway: sin 2θ peaks at 90 degrees, so 45 degrees throws farthest, and angles that add up to 90 share the same range.
