# France in 1774: the empty treasury
In 1774, Louis XVI became king of France at the age of 20. He found an empty treasury, which means the state had no money in its store.
NOTE: France in 1774
- Louis XVI: king at 20, empty treasury
- long wars and the costly court at Versailles
- debt above 2 billion livres, lenders charge 10 per cent interest
- so the state raised taxes (book page 4)
END
Long wars and the costly court at Versailles had drained the money. Helping the American colonies added more than 1 billion livres to the debt. Lenders then charged 10 per cent interest, so the state raised taxes to pay. Takeaway: when debt grows, the state finds more money by taxing. Clear so far?

# The three estates: who was who (Fig. 2)
Imagine a village where your place in society depends on the family you were born into, not on how hard you work. That was France before 1789. Its people were divided into three estates. An estate is a large group or order in society.
NOTE: Three estates
- 1st estate: clergy, the Church
- 2nd estate: nobility
- 3rd estate: everyone else, rich and poor
- privilege came by birth
END
Here is the key idea. Look at Fig. 2 on page 4, and read it from the top. The first estate is the clergy, and the second is the nobility. Both enjoyed privileges simply by birth.
FIGURE slide 4
This diagram shows the three estates, stacked from top to bottom.
First, the clergy at the top, then the nobility.
Next, the third estate, the largest group, split into layers.
Those layers run from big businessmen, merchants and lawyers at the top, to peasants and artisans, and finally small peasants, landless labour and servants at the bottom.
Now take two people. Pierre is a rich merchant in a city, and Marie is a landless labourer in a village. Both belong to the third estate. So both are in the group that pays taxes to the state. Yet Pierre has money and Marie has none. So being in the third estate meant sharing the tax burden, but not sharing wealth.
Quick check: which estate did Pierre belong to, and which estate did the clergy belong to?
Takeaway: the first two estates had privileges by birth, while the third estate carried the tax burden. With me so far?

# Peasants: about 90 per cent of the population, few owning land
Now let's look at the largest group in the third estate, the peasants. Imagine a village of 100 people. About 90 of them are peasants, who farm the land.
NOTE: Peasants
- about 90 per cent of the population
- few owned the land they farmed
- nobles, Church and richer third estate: about 60 per cent of land (book page 4)
END
Here are the facts. Take our village of 100 people. About 90 are peasants, but only a few of those 90 own the land they cultivate. The nobles, the Church and the richer members of the third estate together owned about 60 per cent of the land. So the people who did most of the farming owned the least land. Takeaway: most of the people worked the land, but most of the land belonged to someone else. Make sense?

# Who paid which taxes: tithes, taille, indirect taxes and feudal dues
Who do you think should pay the state's bills? Let's see who really paid.
SKETCH: box:Tithe | arrow:paid to | result:The Church
SKETCH: box:Taille | arrow:paid to | result:The state
SKETCH: box:Salt, tobacco | arrow:taxed on | result:Everyday goods
SKETCH: box:Feudal dues | arrow:owed to | result:The lord
Here is a sketch of the four payments. Look at the first row: a tithe is a tax paid to the Church. It was one-tenth of the agricultural produce. So a peasant who grew wheat handed one part in ten to the Church.
The second row is the taille. It was a direct tax paid to the state, so the peasant paid the state too.
The third row is indirect taxes. These were levied on everyday things like salt and tobacco, so even buying salt meant paying a tax.
The last row is feudal dues. Peasants owed services to the lord, such as work in his house and fields, army service and building roads.
Now the key point. The clergy and nobles were exempt from taxes to the state, by birth. The burden of financing the state was borne by the third estate alone. Takeaway: the people with the least land paid the most. Clear?

# The Spider and the Fly (Fig. 3)
Turn to page 5. This is Fig. 3, The Spider and the Fly. It is an anonymous etching, which means nobody knows who made it.

Look at the top left, where a spider sits in a web near a trapped fly.
Notice how the verses and the web frame the noble as a predator, who feeds on the labour of the poor peasant.
The book reads: "The nobleman is the spider, the peasant the fly." The caption reads: "This poor fellow brings everything, grain, fruits, money, salad. The fat lord sits there, ready to accept it all."
So what does the lord do to earn what the peasant brings? Nothing. He just sits and receives. A spider waits in its web, catches flies, and lives off them without working. The peasant, like the fly, is caught and drained. Your activity on page 5 asks you to explain this choice. Takeaway: the noble takes the peasant's labour without working for it.

# The struggle to survive: population growth and rising bread prices
Imagine you are a peasant with a family to feed, and bread is your main food. What happens when more mouths need the same bread?
NOTE: The struggle to survive
- population: about 23 million in 1715, 28 million in 1789
- more people, more demand for grain
- grain could not keep pace, so bread prices rose
- wages did not keep pace with prices (book page 5)
END
Here are the key facts. The population rose from about 23 million in 1715 to 28 million in 1789. Think of a bakery that bakes the same number of loaves each day, but more customers come every year. Grain production could not keep pace with the demand, so the price of bread rose rapidly. Most workers were labourers whose wages were fixed by their employers. Those wages did not rise as fast as prices. So the gap between the poor and the rich widened. Takeaway: more people with the same food means hungry families. Make sense?

# How a subsistence crisis happens
What do you think happens when a chain of problems starts? Let's see the chain.
FIGURE slide 8
This is a flow diagram of six steps, from the first event to the last.
First, a bad harvest, often caused by drought or hail.
Second, grain becomes scarce.
Third, food prices rise.
Fourth, the poorest can no longer buy bread.
Fifth, weaker bodies and disease epidemics spread.
Sixth, deaths rise and food riots break out.
A subsistence crisis is an extreme situation where the basic means of livelihood are endangered. The textbook says it occurred frequently in France during the Old Regime. Takeaway: one bad harvest can start a chain that ends in starvation and riots. Which link would you break first to stop the chain?

# Recap
Let's tie it together. The state was broke, so it raised taxes. Only the third estate paid them, while the clergy and nobles were exempt. Peasants, about 90 per cent of the people, paid tithes, taille, indirect taxes and feudal dues. More people, too little grain and low wages led to subsistence crises. Back to our question at the start: was it fair? Now you can answer that from the evidence.
